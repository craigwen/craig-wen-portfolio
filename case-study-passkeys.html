Case study · Mastercard, Click to Pay

Getting Click to Pay users to say yes to passkeys

Passkeys replace passwords with biometrics — but only if people trust and understand them enough to turn one on. I led content strategy for the enrollment, management, and confirmation flows, and ran the workshop that got a cross-functional team talking about the same user, in the same terms.

  • My roleEnd-to-end content strategy, workshop facilitation, UX writing
  • CollaboratorsProduct design, product management, and engineering partners on Click to Pay

The situation

Click to Pay is Mastercard's digital wallet for online checkout: save your cards once, then check out with them on any site that accepts Click to Pay, without re-entering card details.

Passkeys are a newer authentication technology built to replace passwords and one-time codes. They let someone sign in with their fingerprint, face, or device PIN instead — and they're phishing-resistant, so they're safer against data leaks than anything password-based. For Mastercard, that meant less fraud and fewer abandoned carts; for shoppers, a faster, more secure checkout.

The ask

Design a flow prompting existing Click to Pay users to create a passkey.

Discovery

Passkeys were new to most of the team, myself included, so before writing anything I got up to speed: read the documentation, created and used passkeys myself, and reviewed Mastercard's own research. Then I benchmarked how other companies were already introducing them — Google, PayPal, and eBay — against FIDO's own UX guidelines for the technology.

Getting everyone aligned

Product, design, and stakeholders each carried different assumptions about what passkeys were and who they were for. Rather than let that surface mid-review, I ran a workshop to get everyone aligned on the right content, for the right person, at the right time — including an exercise to distill our point of view into a single guiding sentence.

From workshop to guidelines

The workshop's output started as rough FigJam stickies. From there, I built a journey map to empathize with the user at each moment of the passkey enrollment flow — what they're trying to do, how they likely feel, and where Mastercard has an opportunity to help rather than interrupt.

I then worked with a research moderator to test directional designs and content against real users, validating or correcting the assumptions baked into those early guidelines. That testing sharpened everything into a small set of content strategy principles:

  • Speak to the consumer's goals, not Mastercard's.
  • Use the consumer's language, not Mastercard's.
  • Be aware of when you're interrupting the consumer.
  • Prioritize motivating action over providing education.
  • Lean on consumers' existing positive attitudes toward biometrics.

Those principles flexed by context — enrollment, management, and legal content all needed their own version of "do" and "don't":

DoHighlight the ability to use biometrics as the primary benefit.
Don'tDisplay educational content defining passkeys during checkout.

The work

Passkey enrollment. Content focuses on how a passkey changes the user's experience, not on the technology itself: the headline leads with biometrics, the description names which biometrics and introduces the word "passkey," and an escape hatch makes clear checkout will continue as usual either way.

Passkey management. This screen isn't interrupting a checkout, so it can afford to include the educational detail enrollment deliberately leaves out.

Confirmation email. Reiterates the primary benefit of using a passkey, with the detail that it replaces the one-time verification code.

Challenges

  • Couldn't use recognizable branded terms like Face ID or Touch ID, because detecting the user's specific device wasn't technically feasible — so the content had to sell biometrics generically, without borrowing that built-in recognition.
  • No opportunity for large-scale in-product A/B testing, which meant leaning more heavily on qualitative user interviews to validate direction before launch.
